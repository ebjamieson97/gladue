Methodology

  1. CollectEvery criminal decision published in the A2AJ Canadian case law dataset for eight courts.
  2. Find the sentencing decisionsA statistical model picks out the decisions that impose or review a sentence.
  3. Read every sentencing decisionAI readers record the offences, the sentence and the mitigating factors.
  4. Choose the extractsFor cases where a systemic factor was treated as mitigating, the crime and the court's reasons are extracted and highlighted.
  5. Check and publishAutomated checks screen each extract before the case appears on the site.

1. The data

The decisions come from the A2AJ Canadian case law dataset, an open collection of published court decisions from the Access to Algorithmic Justice project at York University. The casebook uses the criminal decisions of eight courts: the Supreme Court of Canada; the Courts of Appeal of Ontario, British Columbia, Nova Scotia, and Newfoundland and Labrador; the Supreme Courts of British Columbia and Nova Scotia; and the Provincial Court of Nova Scotia. That is criminal decisions (data as of ).

Only published decisions are included. Most sentences in Canada are delivered orally and never published, so the casebook reflects the decisions courts chose to publish, not every sentence imposed.

2. Finding the sentencing decisions

A logistic regression model scores each criminal decision on 41 features of its text:

  • How often key words and phrases appear (21 counts): "sentence" or "sentencing" overall, and separately in the heading, the opening, the closing and the last 500 characters; aggravating or mitigating factors; sentencing vocabulary; fitness of sentence; pre-sentence and Gladue reports; operative sentencing words ("I sentence", "I impose"); dispositions of a sentence appeal; appeal wording; appeals against conviction; voir dire; bail; guilty pleas; dangerous offender proceedings; "appellant"; "appeal"; "application"; and sentence orders in the closing (probation, DNA order, months, years);
  • Yes-or-no features of the decision (17): whether it comes from a trial court; whether its title reads as a trial or an appeal; whether its heading or opening shows an appeal or an application; whether it cites the sentencing principles of the Criminal Code (s. 718); whether its closing disposes of a sentence; whether the appeal was abandoned; bail wording in the opening or closing; a new trial ordered in the closing; a matter not decided; a procedural opening; a chambers hearing; a guilty plea in the opening; and whether it is a Supreme Court of Canada decision;
  • Shape of the text (3): its length, how densely it mentions sentencing, and how far into the text "sentence" first appears.

Decisions scoring 0.5 or more are treated as sentencing decisions: in all.

The model was trained and tested on two samples of 3,200 decisions (6,084 different decisions), each labelled by AI readers and checked by a second model. On a purely random sample, tested by 5-fold cross-validation, about 95.5% of the decisions it selects are sentencing decisions (precision), and it finds about 94.7% of them (recall). A model was used for this step, rather than AI reading all criminal decisions, because a full reading would have used too many tokens; the model needs only the 6,084 labelled examples, and can be re-run on new decisions at no cost.

A sentencing decision is marked as a trial-court sentencing when it comes from a trial court and its heading shows no appeal; these are the cases where a judge passes sentence on the offender ( decisions). This rule is about 98% precise. The other sentencing decisions are mostly appeal courts reviewing a sentence.

3. Reading every sentencing decision

Every sentencing decision was read by Sonnet 5.5. Decisions up to 18,000 characters were read in full; longer ones as their opening, closing, and the passages about the sentence, mitigating factors and charges. For each decision the reader recorded:

  • the offences, in eleven categories (homicide, sexual offences, assault, robbery, kidnapping, break and enter, theft and fraud, drugs, weapons, driving, other);
  • the outcome (sentence imposed, or an appeal upheld, reduced, increased or varied);
  • the sentence: prison time, conditional sentence, probation, fines and other orders, and any placement in a healing lodge or treatment centre;
  • an exact quotation of the sentence;
  • how the court dealt with each systemic factor, and the ordinary mitigating factors it accepted;
  • whether the decision identifies the offender as Indigenous.

The reading was checked in several ways:

CheckResult
Opus 5.5 independently re-read 150 random decisions: offence categories the same145 of 150
Same check: prison time the same140 of 150
Same check: whether a systemic factor was treated as mitigating147 of 150
Quotations of the sentence found word for word in the decision99.8%

Where two separate readings disagreed about systemic factors (250 decisions), Opus 5.5 re-read a longer text and its answer was used.

4. Systemic factors treated as mitigating

The systemic factors are: Gladue factors (an Indigenous offender's background and the systemic factors affecting Indigenous people), residential schools, colonialism, intergenerational trauma, anti-Black racism, and other racism or prejudice the offender experienced.

5. The extracts

For each case where a systemic factor was treated as mitigating, Sonnet 5.5 chose two passages of the court's own paragraphs: one describing the offence, and one giving the court's reasons on the systemic factors. It also chose the phrases to highlight, following rules refined through several rounds of review:

  • crime highlights show who did what to whom and the harm to the victim, never incidental details or graphic sexual details;
  • the reasons must be the judge's own reasoning; a report's or counsel's words count only if the judge explicitly adopts them;
  • the reasons must show what factor was weighed and the court's conclusion about it;
  • every factor shown as a tag on a case must be visible in its reasons extract.

Named factors such as Gladue, residential schools and colonialism are highlighted automatically. Each extract was then checked automatically for these rules, and failing extracts were re-chosen or left out.

6. Which cases appear on the site

A case is shown when the court deciding it clearly states, in its own words, that the systemic factor mitigated the sentence. Trial-court sentencings and appeals that corrected how the factors were applied are shown on that basis; other appeals are shown when their extracts also describe the crime and a specific factor in the offender's life.

7. Statistics

The Statistics page shows how many decisions involve systemic factors, by province and court, and the number and share of sentencing decisions with a systemic factor clearly treated as mitigating, year by year. Only the cases shown on this site are counted as mitigating.

8. Limitations

  • Published decisions only. Unpublished sentences (courts do not publish all sentences) are not covered, and courts and provinces publish at different rates.
  • Eight courts. Trial-court sentencings come only from British Columbia and Nova Scotia; Ontario is represented by its Court of Appeal only.
